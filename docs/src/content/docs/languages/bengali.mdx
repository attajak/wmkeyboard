Bangla is one of only two languages that ship a dictionary and emoji keywords inside the app itself. English is the other, and Bangla is the only one of the two with a purpose-built phonetic engine on top. It ships seven layouts: the phonetic Avro, the compositional Khipro, and five fixed ones, Probhat, Jatiya, Baishakhi, Bornona, and InScript. Adding Bangla turns on Avro, and the rest are a tap away on its own screen. A phone whose Android language list already includes Bangla gets it enabled from the first run. Type romanized Bangla and the keyboard converts it to script as you go, or pick one of the fixed keyboards if you'd rather type the script directly.

## Typing phonetically with Avro

**Avro phonetic** is a fixed QWERTY grid underneath (every key is exactly where it is on the English layout) with a rule-based transliteration engine layered on top. Type a romanized syllable and it renders in Bengali script directly in the composing text, no separate conversion step: "ami" becomes আমি, "kolokata" becomes কলকাতা, "dhorrmo" becomes ধর্ম. The engine handles conjuncts, reph, khanda-ta, and the vowel/consonant context that decides whether a vowel is a standalone letter or a kar sign. All of that is automatic, so you type the sounds and leave the script mechanics alone.

Where it goes further than a plain transliterator is lenient spelling. Bangla speakers write the same word several different ways in Latin letters (আছি gets typed "achi", "achhi" or "asi" depending on the person), and Avro takes them all. Two layers do that, in this order.

First a hand-built list of about 13,000 romanized spellings, described under [Spellings the rules can't reach](#spellings-the-rules-cant-reach) below. It covers most of the everyday vocabulary, and a spelling it names is settled there and nowhere else, because the list outranks both the dictionary and the literal transliteration. Both `achi` and `asi` are on it, and they're listed separately: `achi` commits আছি, `asi` commits আসি.

Anything the list has never heard of falls to a phonetic fold. Both your romanized input and every word in the Bangla dictionary are reduced to a lenient key where confusable sounds collapse (স/শ/ষ/ছ/চ all fold to the same sound, aspiration like "kh" or "bh" drops to "k"/"b", and so on), so an unlisted spelling still finds the dictionary word it sounds like. The dictionary lists the verb forms of chat Bangla too (বানাইছি, করতিছে, নিছিলাম, আসবিনি, দেখেনাই), so `ashbini` finds আসবিনি and `khujtese` finds খুঁজতেছে. The `s` people type for the ছ sound still gives ছ. Words sharing a key are phonetic siblings, and the most frequent sibling leads. Two things you wrote down are weighed before frequency, because the fold throws them away: a sibling that contradicts an "h" you actually typed is pushed down, and so is one that ends in ও when you typed no final o. The "ch" of চ and the "ph" of ফ are exempt, since those are ordinary English-influenced spellings rather than claims about aspiration.

A sibling only takes the commit from the literal transliteration when it is at least twice as frequent. A near-tie leaves what the composing text already showed you, because a silent swap between two words of roughly equal currency reads as a bug rather than a correction. The exception is a literal that isn't a dictionary word at all: with nothing to protect, the siblings lead. The literal transliteration is always in the suggestion strip as a fallback either way, so you can pick it if the substitution guesses wrong. To stop the substitution altogether, turn off **Correct to dictionary words** (see [Options](#options)).

:::note
Neither layer is part of autocorrect, and neither is controlled by the autocorrect toggle. Avro's phonetic commit runs regardless of whether [Autocorrect](/smart/autocorrect/) is on or off. The fixed-spelling list has its own switch (see **Spellings** under [Options](#options) below). The phonetic fold has none.
:::

A few more of the rules worth knowing:

| You type | You get | Why |
|---|---|---|
| `valo` or `bhalo` | ভাল | "bh" and "v" both fold to the "b" sound |
| `valO` | ভালো | lowercase "o" is the inherent vowel and writes nothing; only capital "O" is ও/ো |
| `kolokata` | কলকাতা | the explicit "o" between l and k breaks the conjunct that `kolkata` (no vowel between them) would form |
| `korbo`, `bolchi` | করব, বলছি | two consonants only join where Bangla writes them as one letter, the same list desktop Avro uses; `kt` is ক্ত, but `rb` and `lch` stay apart |
| `dhorrmo` | ধর্ম | reph is a double r, as on desktop Avro; the common words people spell with one r, like `dhormo`, are on the known-spellings list |
| `onko` / `oncol` | অঙ্ক / অঞ্চল | "n" before k or c takes that letter's own nasal |
| `amio` / `khaoa` | আমিও / খাওয়া | "o" straight after a written vowel is ও |
| `bangla` / `songe` / `baNgali` | বাংলা / সঙ্গে / বাঙালি | ং joins nothing; before a vowel it is ঙ্গ, as on desktop Avro, and capital `Ng` is the bare ঙ |
| `swasthyo` | স্বাস্থ্য | "w" after a consonant is a ব-ফলা join |
| `wasi` | ওয়াসি | "w" anywhere else is ও |
| `panjabi` | পাঞ্জাবি | "n" before "j" is ঞ |
| `hoThaTH`, `hoThat` + a trailing backquote, or `hoThat,,` | হঠাৎ | `TH`, a backquote or a trailing hasant all spell khanda-ta (ৎ) |
| `shyam` | শ্যাম | "y" after a consonant is a য-ফলা join |
| `meye` | মেয়ে | "y" elsewhere is য়, not a join |
| `yes` | ইয়েস | "y" at the start of a word is ইয় |
| `kee` | কী | "ee" is ঈ, or ী after a consonant |
| `aZp` | অ্যাপ | `aZ` is the অ্যা of English loanwords |
| `rZab` | র‍্যাব | after a plain র, য-ফলা keeps the র whole; `karryo` still gives the reph of কার্য |
| `ggan` | জ্ঞান | `gg` is the জ্ঞ conjunct |
| `Bangla` | বাংলা | a capital that spells nothing of its own reads as its lowercase self |
| `$` / `123` | ৳ / ১২৩ | currency and digits convert too |
| `.` / `..` / `...` | । / `.` / `...` | a single dot is দাঁড়ি; two dots is a literal period, and three stay an ellipsis |

Both of Avro's spellings for the marks work, and so do the ones Ridmik uses: ঁ is `^` or `qq`/`cb`, ঃ is `:` or `HH`, ৎ is `t` plus a backquote or `TH`, and the hasant is `,,` or `hs`. The `hs` form only fires after a consonant, where a hasant can actually go, so it never eats the h and s of a name like `ahsan`.

### Spellings the rules can't reach

Some words just aren't spelled the way they sound, so a list of them is checked before the phonetic engine runs. A match there always wins over whatever the transliterator would have produced. Two kinds of word need this:

- **English loanwords.** "Keyboard" is written কিবোর্ড and "chair" চেয়ার by convention, not by sound. Left to the rules, "table" would come out তাব্লে instead of টেবিল. About 2,400 of these are listed by hand.
- **Banglish as people actually type it.** Chat spelling drops vowels wholesale, and no phonetic rule can put back a vowel that was never typed. `tmr` is তোমার, `amk` is আমাকে, `trpr` is তারপর. Colloquial and regional inflections like `boltiso` (বলতিছো) are in the same list, which covers about 12,000 spellings drawn from a corpus of real Bangla chat. So are about 950 words with a reph spelled with a single r, like `dhormo` (ধর্ম) and `porjonto` (পর্যন্ত), since the rules keep a single r apart the way desktop Avro does.

The list stays out of the way where the rules already spell a common Bangla word. `fire` is ফিরে, `nice` is নিচে and `make` is মাকে, as they are in Avro on a computer, and not the English word that happens to share the letters.

| you type | you get | without the list |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `tmr` | তোমার | ত্ম্র |
| `amk` | আমাকে | আম্ক |
| `tmi` | তুমি | ত্মি |
| `table` | টেবিল | তাব্লে |

A listed spelling shows up in the composing text as soon as you finish typing it, not when you hit space: `tmr` reads তোমার at the r. That's the same answer the space would have committed, so nothing changes under you at the last moment.

You can switch this off per language if you'd rather have the letter-for-letter reading (see **Spellings** under [Options](#options) below). See [Downloadable dictionaries](/languages/dictionaries/) for how Bangla's dictionary and these lists fit into suggestions generally.

## Typing with Khipro

**Khipro** (ক্ষিপ্র) is a compositional layout made by the [Khipro team](https://khipro.khiproteam.com/). It is not phonetic. Every key sequence has exactly one meaning, so the keyboard never guesses which word you meant: what you type is what you get, and the space bar commits it exactly as the composing text shows it. There is no known-spellings list and no dictionary substitution on this layout, and autocorrect never touches it. The suggestion strip still helps you finish a word. Its first word is the one you typed, and after it come Bangla words that start with it, from the Bangla dictionary and from words you have typed before.

The grid is QWERTY, with the two changes the Khipro team asks for on a touchscreen. Khipro never uses shift, so the key where shift would be is <KeyCap>/</KeyCap>. The <KeyCap>;</KeyCap> key sits between the spacebar and the দাঁড়ি key. You type everything in lowercase.

Two keys change the letter before them:

- `f` gives a letter's partner: `t` is ত and `tf` is ট, `tff` is ঠ, `df` is ড, `sf` is ষ, `rf` is ড়, and `kf` is ক্ষ.
- `/` is the slicer. It breaks a conjunct back apart (`ks` is ক্স, `ks/` is কস), makes খণ্ড-ত after `t` (`t/` is ৎ), and puts চন্দ্রবিন্দু on the vowel before it (`ca/d` is চাঁদ). Press it twice for a real slash after a vowel.

Two more keys control how letters join:

- `;` separates two parts that would otherwise join or combine: `budh;bar` is বুধবার, where `budhbar` would make a conjunct, and `amar;i` is আমারই. Press it twice for a real semicolon.
- `?` is the blinder. The text after it stays in Latin letters, so `?hello` is hello. Press it twice for a real question mark. Hold <KeyCap>/</KeyCap> for the backslash, which does the same.

The দাঁড়ি key takes `f` too: <KeyCap>।</KeyCap> then `f` is ॥, and `ff` is ৺. Hold the দাঁড়ি key for ॥ directly, and for ZWJ and ZWNJ, the invisible joiners that control how two letters connect.

| You type | You get |
|---|---|
| `ami` | আমি |
| `kobita` | কবিতা |
| `valw` | ভালো |
| `baxladesh` | বাংলাদেশ |
| `kkhipr` | ক্ষিপ্র |
| `dhrrm` | ধর্ম |
| `mae` | ম্যা |
| `du;oo;kh` | দুঃখ |
| `j,,` | জ় |

The complete mapping, with every conjunct, is in the [Khipro documentation](https://khipro.khiproteam.com/documentation/). The keyboard runs the Khipro team's own published definition of the layout (version 36.5.0, with the fixes the Khipro team has published since), so it types the same as Khipro on Linux, Windows and HeliBoard.

On a hardware keyboard, a word you start from the physical keys uses Khipro's desktop version instead, because a physical keyboard has no Bangla digits or দাঁড়ি key: `123` gives ১২৩, `.` gives ।, `..` gives a full stop, and `$` gives ৳. Words you type on the screen keys are not affected.

**Glide typing** works on Khipro too. A stroke over the grid is read as a Khipro spelling and comes out as the Bangla word it spells: swipe `ami` for আমি, `kwthay` for কোথায়. The keyboard works out the Khipro spelling of every word in your Bangla word lists (the bundled one, a downloaded one and any you imported) by running Khipro's own rules backwards and keeping only the spellings Khipro turns back into the word, with the inherent `o` written or left out, so `ekhn` and `ekhon` both reach এখন. Words you have taught the keyboard are included too. The first time, on a downloaded list of 450,000 words, this takes a few minutes in the background, with a notification counting the words as it goes, and swiping starts once it is done; afterwards it is saved and only redone when a list changes. The slicer <KeyCap>/</KeyCap> is part of the swipe: draw through it where চন্দ্রবিন্দু or খণ্ড-ত goes, so `ca/d`-style strokes reach চাঁদ and a stroke ending on <KeyCap>/</KeyCap> after `t` reaches হঠাৎ. Words that need another non-letter key, like the separator <KeyCap>;</KeyCap>, are typed as usual.

Key hints work on Khipro too (see [Options](#options)): with them on, each key shows the Bangla it will add, including the letter that `h` or `f` changes the previous one into.

**Type English words as English** works on Khipro as well. With English picked under **Also suggest from** and the toggle on, keys that spell an English word commit as that word when what they spell in Bangla is not a word the list or your dictionary knows: `hello` stays hello, and `ami` is still আমি. The English word leads the strip, with the Bangla reading right behind it for the times you meant that. Whatever you type, the strip also holds the letters exactly as you typed them, right behind the Bangla reading, so a name or a word no dictionary has can still go in as English with a tap. Khipro has no reading to weigh, so the rule is that plain; it does not use the context scoring the phonetic layout does.

## Choosing a layout

Bangla ships seven layouts. A fresh install turns on one of them, Avro, and only if your phone's own language list has Bangla in it. What starts enabled is read off the phone rather than fixed (see [What ships](/languages/overview/#what-ships)). Turn the rest on whenever you want them, from Bangla's own screen under **Languages**. There's one exception: a keyboard you enabled from Android's settings and then typed on without ever opening the app. The setup wizard never ran, so the built-in fallback stands, which is Avro alongside English QWERTY.

- **Avro phonetic**: the QWERTY grid described above, and the default layout when you add Bangla by searching for it.
- **Khipro**: the compositional layout described above, on QWERTY letters with <KeyCap>/</KeyCap> in place of shift.
- **প্রভাত (Probhat)**: the fixed Bangla layout popular on Linux, following the standard desktop keymap as shipped by Avro Keyboard and OpenBangla. You type Bengali letters directly, with nothing transliterated.
- **জাতীয় (National / Jatiya)**: the Bangladesh national standard fixed layout, also following its desktop keymap.
- **বৈশাখী (Baishakhi)**: another fixed Bangla layout, typed directly with nothing transliterated.
- **বর্ণনা (Bornona)**: another fixed Bangla layout, typed directly with nothing transliterated.
- **ইনস্ক্রিপ্ট (InScript)**: the Bengali InScript layout, the same Indic-script keyboard standard the app ships for many other Indian languages.

On Probhat, shift mostly gives you the aspirated partner of a consonant (দ→ধ, ট→ঠ, গ→ঘ, ক→খ) but not uniformly. Some shift pairs are vowel-length pairs (ি→ই, ু→উ, ী→ঈ) and a few are unrelated letters that just share a key for space reasons (র→ড়, ন→ণ, স→ষ). The 10th key on the home row carries ে with ো on shift and ৈ/ৌ on long-press. A handful of less common marks (ৎ, ঞ, ঁ, ৃ, ৗ, ঽ) live as long-press alternates on a phonetically related key rather than getting a dedicated slot.

Jatiya's letter keys also match its desktop layout. Independent vowels that sit on a desktop AltGr key are reached here as a long-press on their matching kar sign: long-press ু for উ or ঊ, long-press ি for ই or ঈ, and so on.

All seven layouts type দাঁড়ি (।) from the full stop key beside the spacebar rather than a full stop (on Khipro, it comes after the <KeyCap>;</KeyCap> key), with the ASCII `.` moved to that key's long-press for numbers, file names and web addresses. Every layer but the letters is where numbers are typed, so on the <KeyCap>?123</KeyCap> pages and on an Fn, Number or secondary layer the same key stays a plain `.` for decimals and দাঁড়ি moves to its long-press instead. On the five fixed layouts, `?` comes first in that popup and `.` right after it, so a plain hold on the key types a question mark; Avro and Khipro already have one on the hold of <KeyCap>m</KeyCap>.

বিসর্গ (ঃ) sits on the long-press of the colon key on the symbols layer, on all seven. It's drawn as a colon and does a different job, and the symbol layers are one grid shared by every language, so the colon's popup is where it goes.

All five fixed layouts share the same cluster-editing behavior as Avro and Khipro (see [Editing conjuncts and clusters](#editing-conjuncts-and-clusters) below), and the same font choice applies across all seven (see [Options](#options)). Switching between them works exactly like switching between any other enabled layouts: spacebar swipe, hold-drag picker, or the <KeyCap>🌐</KeyCap> key. See [Switching languages](/languages/switching/) for the full mechanics.

## Editing conjuncts and clusters

Bengali script builds conjuncts (যুক্তবর্ণ) by joining consonants with a hasant (্): ক্ষ, স্ত্রী, জ্ঞ. By default, backspace over already-typed Bengali text removes one character at a time, which can leave a half-formed cluster on screen: জ্ঞ with one backspace becomes জ্, not জ.

<SettingsPath path="Languages & layouts / Bangla / Clusters / Conjunct-aware backspace" />

Turning on **Conjunct-aware backspace** changes this: a whole conjunct cluster is deleted in a single press instead. স্ত্রী (a triple conjunct) disappears as one unit, ক্ষ as one unit, and a bare trailing hasant with nothing joined to it yet still deletes alone. The setting is **off by default**, and it applies to committed Bengali-script text across all seven Bangla layouts alike, which share the same cluster logic. It only affects text already committed to the field. Deleting through a word you are still composing on Avro or Khipro always removes one Latin character per press.

This toggle isn't unique to Bangla. Every language on a cluster-forming script gets its own copy of it, and Devanagari, Tamil, Khmer and others work the same way. It's set per language, so Bangla deleting whole conjuncts doesn't commit you to Hindi doing the same.

## Emoji search in Bangla

Bangla is one of the two languages (with English) whose search keywords are baked directly into the bundled emoji catalog, so searching emoji by their Bangla name works with nothing to download. A larger keyword pack for Bangla is downloadable too, and it stacks on top of the bundled set rather than replacing it. ## Writing ANSI (Bijoy) text

Before Unicode, Bangla was typed with Bijoy into fonts like SutonnyMJ, and a lot of design software, print shops and older documents still expect it. That encoding, usually called ANSI, stores each Bangla glyph as a Latin character and leaves the font to draw it as Bangla. আ is stored as `Av`, and আমার সোনার বাংলা as `Avgvi †mvbvi evsjv`.

Turn on **Allow ANSI output** on Bangla's own screen (see [Options](#options)) and every Bangla layout gets an **ANSI** button beside the suggestions: Avro, Khipro and all the fixed layouts. Tap it and everything the keyboard writes in Bangla goes into the text box as ANSI instead of Unicode, whether you typed it, picked it from the strip, swiped it or dictated it. The button stays lit while it's on. Tap it again to go back to Unicode.

- The word you're typing stays in Unicode while it's still being composed, and turns into ANSI when you finish it with a space, a punctuation mark or a pick from the strip. ANSI puts some vowel signs in front of their consonant (কি is `wK`), so a word only converts right once it's whole.
- On a layout that types letter by letter without composing, each letter rewrites the word as you go, so the vowel signs still land in the right place.
- **ANSI version** picks the encoding. Version 2 is the usual one, matching SutonnyMJ and most Bijoy fonts. The fonts don't all put every letter in the same place, so if a conjunct comes out wrong in yours, try version 1 or 3.
- ANSI text is Latin letters as far as the keyboard is concerned. Next-word predictions and taking a correction back with backspace don't work across it, and the keyboard doesn't pick an ANSI word back up for editing when you backspace into it. Backspace removes one ANSI character at a time, the way Bijoy always did.
- ANSI only reads as Bangla in an ANSI font. Everywhere else it's gibberish, so switch the button off when you leave that app.

- **Layouts**: one card each for Avro, Khipro, Probhat, Jatiya, Baishakhi, Bornona, and InScript, each a small preview of that keyboard. Tap a card to turn it on or off; all seven are independent. At least one layout has to stay enabled somewhere across your whole keyboard, so you can't switch off the last one from here.
- **Key hints**: <SettingsPath path="Languages & layouts / Bangla / Key hints / Show the letter each key types" />, a three-way choice (Off, Added, or Whole letter), off by default. It puts the Bengali letter a key is about to write in its corner. Unlike the settings around it, this one is shared. It's one setting for every language with a transliterating layout enabled, not just Bangla, and it shows only while such a layout, Avro or Khipro, is active.
- **Key map**: a folded group just below the hints while Avro is on. Open it for the vowels, consonants, joins and signs, the same tables as the [Avro key map](#avro-key-map) above, so they work offline. **Full guide** at the bottom, <SettingsPath path="Languages & layouts / Bangla / Key map / Full guide" />, opens this page in your browser.
- **Suggestion strip**: <SettingsPath path="Languages & layouts / Bangla / Suggestion strip / Keep the first two words in place" />, off by default, shown while Avro is on. Normally the first word on the strip is the one the space bar will write, so the words move as you type. Turn this on and the first two never move: on the left is what you typed, in English letters, and next to it is the Avro reading of it, letter for letter (`ami` → আমি, `tmr` → ত্ম্র). The suggestions come after them. The space bar still writes the same word as before, and that word is the bold one wherever it sits. A second row, **Suggestions after them**, picks what fills the rest of the strip: **Smart** (both languages, the one the word reads as first, with the other language's best word on the last chip when there is room), **Bengali only**, or **English only**. English only works without English under Also suggest from too, from the bundled English list. While this is on, the strip does not move its top word to the middle.
- **Avro candidate list**: <SettingsPath path="Languages & layouts / Bangla / Suggestion strip / Avro candidate list" />, **Off** by default, shown while Avro is on. Desktop Avro shows a list of words under the word you type, and this is that list, built from desktop Avro's own data (its search rules, its 737 endings and its autocorrect list, which ship with the keyboard, and its dictionary, which the keyboard downloads, about 580 KB, the moment you turn the list on) the way desktop Avro builds it. Until the dictionary is there, the list searches the keyboard's own Bangla words; the row under the setting shows the download and retries it if it failed. Every word desktop Avro would offer is in it, in Avro's order: first Avro's autocorrect spelling for what you typed, then every dictionary word the spelling could be (Avro's search is loose: an `o` can be ো, য় or nothing, so `vodor` finds ভোঁদড় and `dokan` finds দোকান), together with the words made by reading an ending off the end (`bondhuder` → বন্ধু + দের, with Avro's joins: মা + ের is মায়ের), ordered by how close each is to Avro's letter-for-letter reading, and last that reading itself. The keyboard's own Bangla word list is searched the same way, and the keyboard's fixed spellings and own reading are added, so the list can hold words desktop Avro lacks, never fewer than it has. Desktop Avro also remembers which word you picked for a spelling and highlights it next time; this list does not. **In the strip** keeps the word the space bar will write on the first chip and puts the list after it. **Bar above** puts the whole list in a row of its own over the strip, which you scroll sideways; the word the space bar will write is in bold there. The row stays up between words, so the keyboard does not change height as you type. The space bar writes the same word with every choice.
- **Numerals**: two buttons, **০-৯** and **0-9**, and Bengali digits win by default. Switch to `0-9` if you'd rather see Latin digits on the number row while typing Bangla. Where the digits you pick actually get typed is a separate, global setting under Layout & size → Numerals. It defaults to native digits in text fields only, and plain ASCII in number, phone, date and time fields.
- **Also suggest from**: pick other enabled languages to draw secondary suggestions from alongside Bangla. With English picked, Avro's strip also carries the word as you typed it in Latin letters, and an **English words** group appears right below: **Type English words as English** makes the space bar write `hello` as hello, and **Switch on the suggestion strip** shows or hides the small button that turns that on and off while you type. See [English on Avro and Hindi phonetic](/smart/mixed-language/#english-on-avro-and-hindi-phonetic).
- **Dictionary**: Bangla ships with a bundled dictionary, and a downloadable wordlist of about 451,000 words gives better suggestions. You can import your own list too. The downloaded list has no word frequencies of its own, so the bundled list's ranking is kept over it: without that, every sibling would tie and `sorasori` could commit a rarer word that shares its phonetic key instead of সরাসরি. See [Downloadable dictionaries](/languages/dictionaries/) for how sizes compare across languages.
- **Emoji**: download the larger Bangla emoji-keyword pack here, which names about 3,900 emoji. The bundled catalog's Bangla keywords already work without it. The pack arrives on its own if [Download automatically](/emoji/customization/) is on.

- **ANSI output**: <SettingsPath path="Languages & layouts / Bangla / ANSI output / Allow ANSI output" />, off by default. Puts the ANSI button on every Bangla layout, see [Writing ANSI (Bijoy) text](#writing-ansi-bijoy-text). **ANSI version** appears below it once it's on, with three choices and version 2 as the default.
- **Spellings**: <SettingsPath path="Languages & layouts / Bangla / Spellings / Use known spellings" />, on by default. This is the list described above, and it's what makes `table` commit টেবিল rather than তাব্লে, and `tmr` commit তোমার rather than ত্ম্র. Switch it off if you want the letter-for-letter reading instead, which is otherwise unreachable because the list outranks every other suggestion source. The setting is per language, so turning it off for Bangla says nothing about any other script.
- **Dictionary words**: <SettingsPath path="Languages & layouts / Bangla / Spellings / Correct to dictionary words" />, on by default. This is the phonetic fold described above, the step that turns `asi` into আছি when you press space. It runs whether or not autocorrect is on, because it's part of how Avro typing reaches real words. Switch it off and space always commits exactly what the Avro rules spell (`asi` is আসি); the dictionary words stay in the suggestion strip for you to tap. Known spellings are separate, so `tmr` still gives তোমার unless you turn that off too.
- **The words before**: <SettingsPath path="Languages & layouts / Bangla / Spellings / Use the words before" />, on by default. One spelling can stand for several words, and the one or two words you typed just before pick between them, on the strip and for what space writes: "ki pora" decides between পড়া and পরা by what came before. The evidence is your own typing and, where the language has one, the downloaded word-pair data. A known spelling only trades places with another known spelling of the same buffer, so the composing text is still what space writes. Turn it off if a word comes out differently after certain words than you expect.
- **Clusters**: <SettingsPath path="Languages & layouts / Bangla / Clusters / Conjunct-aware backspace" />, covered above, off by default.

One more setting for Bangla lives outside this screen:

- The **Bengali font** row, at <SettingsPath path="Appearance / Keyboard font / Bengali font" />, is one of the per-script font sections. It shows only while a Bengali-script language is enabled, and it's separate from the general keyboard font. Your choice applies to all seven Bangla layouts at once. The section offers the automatic Noto face, eight Bengali-focused Google Fonts, anything in your font library that covers Bengali, and an import button for a `.ttf` or `.otf` of your own. Every script section takes an import, so this isn't Bengali's alone.

## Details & edge cases

**"Banglish" is a different language, not a mode of this one.** Selecting Banglish · Bangla (Romanized) from the language catalogue gives you a plain Latin QWERTY that stays in Latin script (it never converts to Bengali script) and pulls suggestions from its own romanized word list. It has its own emoji keyword pack too: the Bangla keywords in the Latin spellings people chat in (`hasi` and `hashi` both find 😀, `biral` finds the cats, `bhat` and `vat` find 🍚), with the English keywords alongside. That's for people who want to text in Bangla using Latin letters and keep it that way. Avro, covered on this page, is the opposite: it converts what you type into Bengali script. Don't confuse the two if you're looking for one and land on the other in search results.

**A few other languages share the Bengali script** but aren't the same language entry: Assamese, Bishnupriya Manipuri, and Sylheti all use Bengali-script layouts and Bengali-style numerals, but none of them bundle a dictionary the way Bangla does. If you're typing one of those, look for it by its own name in the language catalogue rather than under Bangla.

**The near-tie protection can feel inconsistent if you don't know it's there.** Because a phonetic sibling only overrides the literal transliteration when it's at least twice as frequent, two words that are close in frequency won't always resolve the way you expect. This is deliberate, so the keyboard never silently swaps in a different word than what the composing preview showed you a moment before you hit space. It only applies to spellings the fixed list doesn't already name, since a listed spelling settles the commit before frequency is consulted at all.

**The layout editor shows both shift labels at once for Probhat and Jatiya keys**, even though the real keyboard only shows one label per key state. Many fonts render a bare vowel sign (া, ি) as an orphaned, hard-to-read mark on its own, so the editor shows the base and shift labels side by side purely so you can tell keys apart while editing. This doesn't change what's drawn on the actual keyboard.

**A rearranged Bangla layout still types Bangla.** If you open Probhat, Jatiya, or Avro in the [layout editor](/languages/custom-layouts/) and move keys around, the result still draws suggestions from the Bangla dictionary and still composes Bengali script. A layout's language comes from what it's based on, not from its key arrangement.
